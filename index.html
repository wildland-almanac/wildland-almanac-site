The Wildland Almanac

An open, university-developed 30 m record of how managed and unmanaged forests, shrublands, and grasslands change — vegetation, carbon, water, fuels, fire hazard, and disturbance. California is released now; the contiguous U.S. follows.

California released 30 m 41 years 19 properties CC BY updated annually

The Wildland Almanac is a reconciled record of how managed and unmanaged forests, shrublands, and grasslands change over time. It is derived from the Landsat archive at 30 m resolution and brings nineteen biophysical properties — vegetation structure, carbon, water balance, fuels and fire behavior, disturbance, and drought vulnerability — into one internally consistent datacube. The layers are reconciled together rather than stitched from separate single-purpose products, so the tradeoffs between carbon, water, fire, and habitat can be read in one place. California is released now, covering water years 1985–2025; the contiguous U.S. follows.

Managing wildlands for fire, water, carbon, and habitat is enormous and enduring work, and much of it is already done well — by skilled GIS analysts and planners in agencies, tribes, NGOs, and conservancies who bring deep experience and local knowledge to it. We meet that work where it already happens. The Almanac streams directly into the tools and workflows these analysts already use, and makes their work faster and better grounded. Better data and better models matter, but on their own they tend to stall before they reach a decision; the data has to arrive where the decision is actually made.

That decision is made by the analyst. Software will take on more of the work over time, and we mean to help build it — but the highest-value step today is to amplify the analysts who already do this work: to give them a deeper, more consistent evidence base to reason from, and to build steadily from there. That is what the Almanac is for.

Open under CC BY · cloud-native (COG + STAC), built to stream directly into ArcGIS, QGIS, and other places where geospatial work is done · developed from UC Irvine Earth System Science research · updated every year.

What it covers

The Almanac maps the vegetated, non-irrigated land surface of the contiguous U.S. — managed and unmanaged forests, shrublands, and grasslands, from the backcountry to the wildland–urban interface. It includes intermix and interface areas, managed forests and plantations, and stands recently thinned, cut, or burned.

The data are produced wall‑to‑wall and then masked to where Landsat‑derived properties are reliable. It does not cover open water, urbanized land, or intensively managed cropland.

The mechanism, end to end. The Almanac lives once — documented here at WildlandAlmanac.org, served from the data store on Source Cooperative — and streams through clean, cloud-native pipes into the tools analysts already use. Skilled analysts and planners turn that stronger evidence base into decisions on the ground, and what they learn feeds back to improve the record. The analyst is at the center of the loop, not bypassed by it.
